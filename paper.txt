                                                                                               Abstract
                                                     Conditional density estimation describes how the entire distribution of a response changes
                                                 with covariates, and in imaging studies also with location. Logistic Gaussian processes (LGP)
                                                 give a flexible prior for such densities. However, the normalizing constant of an LGP has no
                                                 closed form, so existing methods approximate or replace the likelihood. We propose the exact
                                                 likelihood finite-rank LGP (ExFR-LGP), which writes the log density as the sum of two bivariate
                                                 functions, one of the response and a location-varying linear index of the covariates, and one of
                                                 the response and the location. Each function is assigned a finite-rank Gaussian process prior that
                                                 is piecewise linear on a regular grid, enabling the normalizing constant, the conditional mean
                                                 and the quantiles to enjoy closed form representations. Posterior samples are drawn from the
                                                 exact posterior under this prior via a Gibbs sampler that updates the Gaussian components by
                                                 elliptical slice sampling. When the true log density is the sum of two such bivariate functions, we
                                                 show that the posterior contracts at the minimax rate of an α-smooth bivariate density up to a
                                                 logarithmic factor. Numerical experiments using synthetic data demonstrate the effectiveness of
                                                 ExFR-LGP in conditional density estimation. Furthermore, application to fractional anisotropy
                                                 responses along the corpus callosum in the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative data
                                                 yields covariate-adjusted percentile bands with uncertainty, elucidating how diagnosis changes
                                                 the distribution along the tract.

1     Introduction

In diverse fields such as biology (Beaumont, Zhang, and Balding, 2002), cosmology (Rau et al.,

2015), ecology (Wood, 2010), and economics (Kalda and Siddiqui, 2013), there is a pressing need for

modeling the distribution of a dependent variable given the covariates, rather than merely modeling

conditional mean or variance. Such problems are referred to as conditional density estimation (CDE),

where given a response-covariate pair (y, x) ∈ R × Rp , one aims to find the density function of y

conditioned on x, denoted by f (y | x).

    As a motivating example, we consider the data from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initia-

tive (ADNI) study (Jack et al., 2008). For each individual, a summary measure called the Fractional

Anisotropy (FA) measuring the brain connectivity (Özarslan, Vemuri, and Mareci, 2005) is calcu-

lated at varying locations across the skeleton of the midsagittal corpus callosum, along with different

features including age, sex, and handedness. As demonstrated in Figures 1a and 1b, the FA mea-

surements vary not only with respect to individuals, but also according to measurement locations.

Based on the FA measurements, clinically relevant questions include where along the corpus callosum

an individual’s FA values fall outside a covariate-adjusted reference percentile band, and how exten-

sive these departures are. Therefore, our main interest is to model an individual’s FA measurement

through the conditional density f (y | x, s) of the FA value y given the covariates x and the location

s along the tract. The fitted density should support uncertainty quantification for clinically rele-

vant summaries, such as covariate-adjusted percentile curves, and should also indicate how multiple

covariates and location alter the FA distribution.

    Quantile regression targets such percentile bands directly, and Zhang et al. (2022) fit the quantiles

of FA along the same corpus callosum skeleton in ADNI (see also Reich, Fuentes, and Dunson, 2011).

Each quantile is then linear in the covariates, and other distributional properties such as density,

modes, and tail probabilities must be derived from the fitted quantile curves. To address this, one

possible approach is parametric distributional regression (Rigby and Stasinopoulos, 2005; Klein et al.,

modes is limited by that family, which might inhibit flexible density modeling.

   CDE addresses this problem by nonparametrically modeling the density function so that the

relevant properties such as tail probabilities and modes can be obtained directly from the density

estimate. CDE has a well-established frequentist literature, including kernel (Chen, 2017) and local

polynomial (Fan, Yao, and Tong, 1996) methods, alongside a growing body of Bayesian approaches

(McDonald and Campbell, 2021). One Bayesian approach models the joint density of (y, x) and

obtains the conditional density from it. This approach requires modeling the covariate distribution,

including the four binary covariates in our application (sex, handedness, AD status, and MCI status).

Moreover, when several covariates are included, clustering may be driven primarily by x rather than

by y (Wade et al., 2014). The other route models f (y | x) directly, mostly as a mixture, whose

weights or atoms depend on the covariates (Dunson, Pillai, and Park, 2007; Griffin and Steel, 2006;

Dunson and Park, 2008), with spatial versions (Gelfand, Kottas, and MacEachern, 2005; Reich and

Fuentes, 2007) and, for responses on [0, 1], dependent Bernstein polynomial priors (Petrone, 1999;

Barrientos, Jara, and Quintana, 2017). Mixtures are designed for heterogeneity that arises from

latent subpopulations. In the FA data, the main sources of heterogeneity are observed covariates



                                                  3
and the density changes gradually along the tract (Figure 1a). In a mixture, such a change is spread

over the weights and atoms, so that the effect of a single covariate on it does not correspond to a

parameter of the model.

   For this purpose, the logistic Gaussian process (LGP; Leonard, 1978; Lenk, 1988) has been widely

studied. It writes the density as exp {Z(y)} divided by its integral over the response space, where

Z is modeled as a Gaussian process (GP). Since its exponential integration does not have a closed

form, existing LGP methods largely fall under two classes. The first discretizes the GP and computes

the integral numerically. It tracks the process at a finite set of nodes (Tokdar, 2007; Tokdar, Zhu,

and Ghosh, 2010), bins the data with a Laplace approximation (Riihimäki and Vehtari, 2014; Payne

et al., 2020), or represents the GP by random Fourier features (Gautier and Ginsbourger, 2026) or by

piecewise multilinear finite elements on a uniform grid (Hegland, 2007; Griebel and Hegland, 2010).

For example, Jacobs (2014) discretizes an LGP on a Riemannian manifold. The second class avoids

the normalizing constant. Adams, Murray, and MacKay (2009a) pass the GP through a bounded link

and treat the rejected proposals of a rejection sampler as latent variables, with extensions to Poisson

process intensities (Adams, Murray, and MacKay, 2009b) and Pólya-Gamma augmentation (Donner

and Opper, 2018; Wang, 2024). Others replace the likelihood by a score matching loss (Chen et al.,

2026; Paisley, Zhang, and Barr, 2025).

   Such LGP methods, however, face two limitations in our modeling context. The first class com-

putes the integral numerically even after discretizing the GP, so the resulting likelihood is an approx-

imation. The second class changes the link and adds latent points whose number grows with n, or

replaces the likelihood. In each case, moments and quantiles of the fitted density need numerical inte-

gration. Prediction-wise, for estimating a conditional density given a p-dimensional covariate vector

x, the minimax rate for an α-smooth conditional density is n−α/(2α+p+1) , which deteriorates quickly

as p grows (Tsybakov, 2009). Tokdar, Zhu, and Ghosh (2010) reduce dimensionality by projecting

x onto a low-dimensional subspace, yielding faster posterior contraction when the true conditional

density depends on x only through such a (low-rank) projection (Tokdar, 2011), while Odin, Bachoc,

and Lagnoux (2026) extend such rates for regression and density estimation to dimensions that grow

with n. Such projection LGP methods, however, still approximate the normalizing constant.

   In this paper, we propose the exact likelihood finite-rank LGP (ExFR-LGP), a finite-rank LGP

g1 captures covariate effects through a linear index with location-dependent coefficients, while g2

the rate n−α/(2α+2) up to a log factor, which is the minimax rate for an α-smooth bivariate density

and the rate of Tokdar (2011) under a rank one projection. Since the grid values have a Gaussian

prior induced by the GP, we sample them by the elliptical slice sampler (ESS; Murray, Adams, and

2.2    Related and alternative models

Several existing models share parts of this construction. The index is a one-dimensional version of

the subspace projection LGP (spLGP) of Tokdar, Zhu, and Ghosh (2010), which models f (y | x) as

a function of (B T x, y) for a p × d matrix B and selects d from the data. Here, B is common to all x

and the model has no location component. There is a literature utilizing finite representations of the



                                                     8
GP used in LGP, such as orthogonal series (Verdinelli and Wasserman, 1998). However, its integral

does not enjoy a closed form.

    The representation (5) is closest to that of Griebel and Hegland (2010), who compute the posterior

mode of an LGP density by a Galerkin approximation in piecewise multilinear functions on a uniform

grid, evaluate its integrals by Gauss quadrature and bound the discretization error. ExFR-LGP uses

the same elements in two dimensions to define the prior, such that the likelihood is exact, enabling

sampling of the whole posterior.

    Gautier and Ginsbourger (2026) and Chen et al. (2026) allow the density to vary with location

by taking the location as an input of the GP, and fit it by numerical integration and by a score

matching loss, respectively. In Sections 5 and 6, we also compare ExFR-LGP with a full grid LGP,

which keeps the finite-rank construction of ExFR-LGP but replaces g1 (y, wx,s ) + g2 (y, s) in (3) by a

single function g(y, x, s) and represents it by linear interpolation on a tensor grid in (y, x, s), which

is the discretization of Griebel and Hegland (2010) applied to (y, x, s). We also compare it with the

spLGP of Tokdar, Zhu, and Ghosh (2010) and the logit stick-breaking mixture of Rigon and Durante

(2021).

7     Conclusions

We proposed ExFR-LGP, a finite-rank LGP model for conditional densities that change with covari-

ates and location. The normalizing constant of an LGP is the integral of an exponentiated GP, which

has no closed form. We approximate the GP by linear interpolation on a regular grid, resulting in a

log density that is piecewise linear in the response, and the likelihood, the mean and the quantiles

available in closed form. We reduce dimensionality by expressing the log density, up to a normal-

izing term, as the sum of two bivariate functions: one of the response and a covariate index with

location-dependent coefficients, following a single-index formulation, and the other of the response

and location. The posterior contracts at the minimax rate of a bivariate α-smooth function up to a

logarithmic factor when the true log density is the sum of two such bivariate functions. Simulation

studies revealed that ExFR-LGP was more accurate than a full grid LGP and spLGP with the same

number of MCMC iterations, and comparable to or more accurate than LSBP. Application to the

ADNI data generated covariate-adjusted percentile bands of FA along the corpus callosum.

    The current model has several limitations. Conditional independence across locations may un-

derstate posterior uncertainty when within-individual responses are strongly correlated along the

tract. The single-index structure also restricts covariate effects at each location to one linear com-

bination, while the posterior contraction result requires the true log density, up to normalization,

to admit the specified additive bivariate representation. Future work will investigate multiple-index

extensions (Xia et al., 2002) to accommodate richer covariate effects, and Gaussian copula models

                                                 22
to capture within-individual dependence separately from the covariate-adjusted FA distributions at

individual locations (Hoff, 2007), retaining their closed-form likelihood contributions. Additional

directions include establishing posterior contraction under dependent responses, developing simulta-

neous reference bands for tract-level FA profiles, and quantifying uncertainty in summaries such as

the proportion of locations at which an individual’s FA falls outside the reference band. The clinical

utility of these summaries will require validation against clinical outcomes.

C.2 Coverage of the credible band

For each test individual x and location s, the coverage of the band is the probability, under Y ∼

f0 (· | x, s), that the band contains f0 (Y | x, s), and we average it over the test individuals and the

locations. Averaged over all settings of Section 5, the coverage is 0.92 and 0.87 for ExFR-LGP in

Case 1 and Case 2, against 0.84 and 0.82 for LSBP, 0.58 and 0.55 for the spLGP and 0.22 and 0.30

for the full grid LGP.
